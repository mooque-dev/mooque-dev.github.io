23:T1e51,<h2 id="before-any-of-this-was-a-job">Before Any of This Was a Job</h2>
<p>I wanted to be a children's book illustrator. High school art class was pencil, watercolour, and a Monami pen, and the pen drawing below is still the thing I'm proudest of making with my hands. One pencil piece ran in the Hamilton Spectator before I ever touched a computer for design.</p>
<figure class="phones">
  <img src="/illustration/paint/hamilton-spectator.jpg" alt="Pencil drawing of a recycling bin, published in the Hamilton Spectator" />
  <img src="/illustration/paint/mosasaur.jpg" alt="Detailed pen and ink drawing of a mosasaur, with hand-lettered title" />
  <figcaption>A pencil still life that ran in a newspaper, and the Monami pen piece I still can't believe I had the patience for</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>A few years later, in design school, most of what I made was practice: redrawing characters I didn't own, in a style I was still building. It's fan art, not original work, but it's where the character-drawing instinct that later shaped ARND and De Mello actually came from.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="/illustration/graphic-design/character-practice.jpg" alt="Four illustrated character portraits in a geometric pattern style: Nemo, Chicken Little, Stitch, and Winnie the Pooh, redrawn as practice" />
  <figcaption>Practice work, redrawing existing characters in my own line style</figcaption>
</figure>
<h2 id="arnd-drawing-the-three-audiences-before-building-their-screens">ARND: Drawing the Three Audiences Before Building Their Screens</h2>
<p>Before I wireframed ARND's onboarding, I drew the three people the app actually serves: a fan who wants to find a show tonight, an artist who wants to be found, and a venue or talent scout looking for who to book. Drawing them as characters first, instead of describing each one in a paragraph, is what made the onboarding this simple: pick the character that looks like you, get a path built for that person.</p>
<figure class="phone">
  <img src="/illustration/arnd/onboarding.jpg" alt="ARND onboarding screen: pick who you relate to most, with three illustrated characters for Music Enthusiast, Artist, and Looking for Talent" />
  <figcaption>The three characters became the three onboarding paths, not the other way around</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>The same instinct shaped how ARND looks outside the app. Street performance photography is the whole marketing story, buskers, pop-up sets, a folding chair and a guitar case, and a straight rectangular crop makes all of it look like stock photography. So I drew a repeating organic shape, tested at a few scales, and used it to crop and frame the real photos instead.</p>
<figure class="phones">
  <img src="/illustration/arnd/marketing-crop.jpg" alt="Street performance photo cropped through a custom organic flower-shaped mask" />
  <img src="/illustration/arnd/shape-study.jpg" alt="The same shape tested as a repeating pattern in different fill treatments, solid and marbled" />
  <figcaption>One drawn shape, tested as a crop, a pattern, and a texture, before it became the app's visual signature</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>Not every drawing here is a finished asset. This one is a share-card shape I was testing for a moment in the app where there's nothing to say yet, a placeholder that admits it's a placeholder instead of a default gray box.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="/illustration/arnd/ticket.jpg" alt="A ticket-shaped illustration with the placeholder text: some random text here because I'm too shy to share" />
  <figcaption>An empty state that's honest about being empty, drawn instead of defaulted</figcaption>
</figure>
<h2 id="a-personal-zine">A Personal Zine</h2>
<p>Outside of client and product work, I write and lay out a small personal zine, essays about daily life in Toronto, in Korean, paired with photography and hand lettering. There's no product metric attached to this one. It's where I keep the muscle that doesn't get used in a Figma file: drawing type by hand until it says something a typeface can't.</p>
<figure class="wide">
  <img src="/illustration/yuwol/zine-spread.jpg" alt="Zine spread with hand-lettered bubble type reading Why do all the vintage stores smell the same, next to a black and white photo and an essay" />
  <figcaption>Hand-lettered title type, drawn for one specific essay and never reused</figcaption>
</figure>
<h2 id="a-hundred-days-of-small-drawings">A Hundred Days of Small Drawings</h2>
<p>I did Dribbble's 100-day UI challenge twice, in 2018 and in 2020. Most of it was interface practice, buttons, type pairings, layout rhythm. But the days that held up were the ones with a small illustrated moment in them: an icon that's a joke instead of a glyph.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="/case-studies/dribbble-ui-challenge/08.jpg" alt="Music app interface with a playlist icon illustrated as a cat playing a trumpet, labeled Cat Trumpet" />
  <figcaption>A hand-drawn icon where a stock music-note glyph would have done the job, just not as well</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>The full 200 days are at <a href="https://dribbble.com/mooque_">dribbble.com/mooque_</a> and in <a href="/work/dribbble-ui-challenge">the case study</a>. A later, more finished set lives in a Figma file I'm glad to walk through directly, since the prototype link sits behind a login.</p>
<h2 id="de-mello-one-character-every-room-it-has-to-work-in">De Mello: One Character, Every Room It Has to Work In</h2>
<p>De Mello makes the case clearest. The rebrand's anchor is a single line-drawn tiger, and the hard design problem was never drawing it once. It was making one character do a brand's entire job: hero illustration, product packaging, social templates, and the small transactional moments a coffee roaster actually sends, a birthday note, a holiday card, an apology.</p>
<figure class="phone">
  <img src="/illustration/de-mello/hero.jpg" alt="De Mello website hero with the tiger illustration and coffee bag product photography" />
  <figcaption>The character carries the homepage before a single product photo does</figcaption>
</figure>
<figure class="phones">
  <img src="/illustration/de-mello/tiger-mark.png" alt="The De Mello tiger drawn as a simple line-art mark" />
  <img src="/illustration/de-mello/hover-tiger.jpg" alt="The same tiger, peeking into a subscription product card from the top and bottom" />
  <figcaption>The same lines, first as a standalone mark, then peeking into a product card</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>The social content system reused the same character as the entry point for an origin story about the brand, not just a logo watermark.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="/illustration/de-mello/content-template.jpg" alt="Social media content template featuring the tiger illustration on a coffee bag, with a headline about drinking the coffee for superpowers" />
  <figcaption>The character fronting the brand's origin story instead of a product shot</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>The gift cards are where the range shows up clearest. Same tiger, three completely different jobs: celebratory, sentimental, and self-aware enough to be funny about a mistake.</p>
<figure class="phones">
  <img src="/illustration/de-mello/gift-birthday.jpg" alt="Birthday gift card with the tiger wearing a party hat, reading: I'll give you my superpowers for your birthday" />
  <img src="/illustration/de-mello/gift-christmas.jpg" alt="Christmas gift card with the tiger wearing a Santa hat, reading: Merry Christmas" />

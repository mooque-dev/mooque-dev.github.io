<p>I talked to 15 nonprofit operations managers and expected to hear about complexity concerns. Instead, the #1 fear was much simpler:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>"What if I accidentally email <mark class="pen" data-note="the fear that shaped the product">all 5,000</mark> of our donors?"</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That insight reshaped the entire design. The problem wasn't complexity. It was confidence. People needed to trust the system before they'd use it.</p>
<p>I also studied the competitive landscape by testing Hubspot and Mailchimp directly using Keela's internal accounts, finding strengths and weaknesses, not just feature lists. I analyzed Phrasee, Klaviyo, Simplero, and ActiveCampaign. The research surfaced user stories we hadn't initially captured and exposed real gaps in competitor approaches around re-enrollment logic and contact-level control.</p>

<p>The easy version of this feature ships the workflow builder alone and calls it done, since that's the part everyone asked for on the roadmap. I decided the builder wasn't the hard problem. The hard problem was getting someone who had never trusted software with donor data to press activate. Every decision below answers to that, not to feature completeness.</p>
<h3>Visual Workflow Builder</h3>
<p>A node-based canvas that makes automation logic tangible. Users see their automation as a visual flow (trigger, conditions, actions) instead of filling out abstract forms. The builder deliberately constrains complexity: start from a template, extend it. No blank canvas anxiety.</p>
<h3>Preview Mode (The Key Feature)</h3>
<p>Before activating any automation, users can preview exactly which contacts it would affect. This was the feature that gave people the courage to actually turn things on. They could verify without risk. This design decision directly traces back to the 5,000-donor fear uncovered in research.</p>
<figure class="wide">
  <img src="/case-studies/automation-nonprofits/untitled.png" alt="Final automation workflow builder, node-based canvas with triggers, conditions, and actions" />
  <figcaption>The final product: visual workflow builder with branch logic and preview mode</figcaption>
</figure>
<h3>Templated Journeys</h3>
<p>Rather than a blank canvas, users start from pre-built journey types: New Donor Journey, Recurring Donor Journey, Disengaged Donor Journey. Each template suggests trigger timing and email structure: not the copy, but the scaffolding. Getting the scaffolding right is what most organizations struggle with.</p>
<h3>Progressive Complexity</h3>
<p>New users see a curated template library. As they build confidence, the interface reveals more: custom triggers, conditional logic, multiple branches. The system grows with the user.</p>

<p>Automation was the second project I was given after joining Keela. It was first scoped at three weeks, too tight to interview users, test, and validate properly. Frequent check-ins with the PM and the lead designer made it clear the deadline would cut the value of the feature. After more conversations with everyone involved, we earned the time to extend it, and the feature that shipped was loved by individual users and organizations alike. The extra time was worth it.</p>
<p>On a personal note, this was the first project where I accepted that collaboration makes a better product. I had been stuck thinking every task was mine to finish alone, and I was afraid to ask for help. Automation is one of my proudest projects, and it couldn't have been built without the people credited below.</p>

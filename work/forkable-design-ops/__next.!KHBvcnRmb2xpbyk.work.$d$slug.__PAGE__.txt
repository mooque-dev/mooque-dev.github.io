<p>I was told early on that design system investment wasn't worth it at our scale. I disagreed.</p>
<p>Startups that only focus on delivering tend to build on faulty foundations. When a problem surfaces, it's rooted deep. At some point, teams accept the debt and keep building on top of it. Companies that invest in systems early accept slower initial velocity in exchange for compounding returns.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="/case-studies/forkable/untitled-5.png" alt="Diagram showing faulty foundation pattern, startups delivering on top of broken systems" />
  <figcaption>The faulty foundation pattern: most startups recognize this only after the cost compounds</figcaption>
</figure>
<figure>
  <img src="/case-studies/forkable/untitled-6.png" alt="Diagram showing companies with proper foundations, systems that evolve with the product" />
  <figcaption>The alternative: systems that evolve with the product and reduce maintenance cost over time</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>I used Toss as an example: one of South Korea's fastest-growing fintechs spent months refining their design system and saved roughly 1,000 hours of design and development time in the six months after adopting it.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="/case-studies/forkable/untitled-7.jpg" alt="Toss design system ROI chart, maintenance cost vs product scale, showing systems investment paying off" />
  <figcaption>Toss: 1,000 hours saved in 6 months after adopting a proper design system. Y-axis: maintenance cost, X-axis: product scale.</figcaption>
</figure>

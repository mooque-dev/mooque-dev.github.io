<p>It worked: we got two dedicated frontend engineers, the first time a design system had earned that.</p>
<p>I ran the audit myself over about three weeks and tracked it in Figma and Notion. Engineers from all three products helped me find the one-offs and inconsistencies that were really in the code, and told me what worried them about building the shared versions. Most of the 340+ turned out to be the same thing built more than once. The dropdown alone existed in nine variations. They became one select with four variants (single, multiple, searchable, and with icons) plus a separate date picker. Address and country lookups became the searchable variant with preset lists, and the two that only looked like dropdowns went away.</p>

<p>The call had a cost. My components were roomier and friendlier, and the Aplos team pushed back hardest: on their accounting screens that cost space and got in the way of workflows people already knew. They also had a fair point about tone: accounting software that looks playful doesn't serve the people who trust it with their books. So Orchid kept one set of components and carried a variation for each product, Keela, Raisely and Aplos, plus a neutral white-label one, with usage guidelines and notes on where each component was used. In the end Aplos adopted most of it.</p>

<p>I had no authority over most of the people who needed Orchid, so adoption had to be earned: weekly office hours, pairing with engineers during migration, a migration guide per codebase, and bi-weekly stakeholder reviews. Teams adopted it because it made their work faster.</p>
<p>I got governance wrong first. Contributing meant filling in a form and bringing it to a weekly meeting, and I was building an approval step on top. Proposals dropped to near zero. The signs were everywhere once I looked: an empty queue, the same questions passed along in broken-telephone threads across Slack channels, no single source of truth, and engineers quietly building their own components. <mark class="pen" data-note="my mistake, and I fixed it">The formality was the problem</mark>, so I dropped the approval step and rebuilt around 48-hour async reviews and light templates. Proposals doubled.</p>
<p>Orchid was adopted across all three products, four engineering teams now build with it, and it is the foundation of the unified experience. Along the way it earned two dedicated front-end engineers, and contribution proposals doubled once reviews went async.</p>
